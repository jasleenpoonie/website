Bank of England MPC rate recommendation

Monetary policy note
For the 17 December 2026 MPC
Hold at 4.00%with hawkish guidance, against an ~80% market-implied hike

Background

The MPC raised Bank Rate by 25bp to 4.00% on 5 November 2026 by an 8–1 vote, with one member voting for 50bp. Rate futures now price an ~80% chance of another hike in December and two more by June 2027.

Why I lean towards holding

October CPI rose to 3.6%, but the increase came from record diesel prices and an earlier tobacco duty rise. Core inflation stayed at 2.8%, and regular pay growth slowed from 3.7% to 3.5%. To me this looks mostly like an energy supply shock, which interest rates can do little to fix, so I think there is a good case for patience.

What could prove me wrong

Services inflation is sticky at 3.7% and unemployment fell to 4.6%. If either feeds into wage-setting, second-round effects could follow. So my suggestion keeps a future rise firmly on the table, and I'm looking forward to seeing how the December decision compares.

Taylor rule model of the UK policy rate

Quantitative model

Method

A simple Taylor rule, which I ran across neutral real rates of 0.5 to 1.0%, output gaps of −0.5 to 0% and three inflation measures (headline, core and services CPI). The model above is live, so please do try changing the inputs.

Finding

Core inflation implies a policy rate of 3.5 to 4.2%, which brackets today's Bank Rate. Headline implies 4.7 to 5.4%. One way to read this: markets seem to expect the MPC to respond to the energy shock rather than look through it. It was really interesting to see how much the answer depends on which inflation measure you choose.

Relative value trade idea: UK gilt curve

Rates trade idea
DV01-neutral 2s10s steepener

The trade

Buy 2-year gilts and sell 10-year gilts, weighted so each side has the same sensitivity to yields (DV01-neutral). A parallel move in yields leaves the position flat; it makes money only if the curve steepens.

Why it should work

The 2-year yield moves most with expectations for Bank Rate, and it is priced for further hikes. If the MPC holds in December, those expectations come out of the front end, 2-year yields fall more than 10-year yields, and the curve steepens.

What could go wrong

The idea would lose money if higher oil prices feed into wages and the Bank raises rates more than expected. Exit triggers: regular pay growth back above 4%, or core CPI above 3%.

M&A valuation: EQT / Intertek £10.9bn take-private

Sell-side M&A project
Jul – Sep 2026

The question

Private equity firm EQT agreed to take Intertek private at £61.08 a share, a £10.9bn enterprise value, after a 4-round bid process. I wanted to see for myself whether the offer was fair to Intertek's shareholders.

Method

I valued the company three ways. Trading comparables gave an enterprise value of £9.9–10.7bn. Precedent transactions gave a view of what buyers had paid for similar businesses. A 5-year unlevered DCF in Excel, using a 7.5% CAPM-derived WACC, landed within 3% of the final deal terms.

Recommendation

All three approaches supported the price, so I concluded the £61.08 offer looked fair and practised writing it up as a board memo.

Sum-of-the-parts: Associated British Foods

Equity and strategy project
Jul – Aug 2026

The question

Associated British Foods combines Primark with grocery, sugar, agriculture and ingredients businesses. I was curious whether the market was valuing the group at less than the sum of its parts.

Method

I built a 5-segment sum-of-the-parts model, valuing each business on peer multiples and applying 35–45% discounts. The parts added up to £21.1bn against a £14.7bn market capitalisation, a 44% gap.

Catalyst

A demerger separating Primark from the food businesses (FoodCo) seemed to me the most likely way to close the gap, by letting investors value each on its own terms.

What I learned making markets at the Optiver Trade-a-thon

24 Nov 2026

Earlier this term I took part in the Cambridge University x Optiver Trade-a-thon, run with the Cambridge Marshall Society. We traded a single stock live on Optibook's DeltaDash market making simulator, reacting to company-specific, probabilistic, market and general news. It was fast, a little chaotic and great fun. Here are my five takeaways.

1. Quantitative analysis comes first

Turning each headline into an expected value quickly was the foundation of every good trade. When I did the maths first, I traded calmly; when I didn't, I guessed.

2. Manage your spread

Quoting both sides earned steady profit between news events. Most of the time, being a reliable market maker mattered more than being clever.

3. Read the room

Anticipating how others would react, and overreact, was as valuable as the maths. Prices move on what people believe, not only on what is true.

4. Trade towards a flat book

Controlling my position stopped one bad move from undoing my gains. It's the trading version of not putting all your eggs in one basket.

5. Know when to take risk

Leaning into my spread more aggressively towards the end made the difference in the rankings. Being careful all day is what made it possible to be bold at the right moment.

A big thank you to Optiver and the Cambridge Marshall Society for a brilliant event. I'd love to do it again.

Why I think the Bank of England can afford to wait

20 Nov 2026

In November the Bank of England raised interest rates to 4.00%, and markets now think another rise in December is very likely. I've been trying to work out whether I agree, and I keep landing on the same answer: the Bank can probably afford to wait.

What's pushing inflation up?

Headline inflation rose to 3.6% in October, but most of the increase came from record diesel prices and a tobacco duty rise. Core inflation, which strips out energy and food, stayed at 2.8%, and regular pay growth actually slowed to 3.5%. That looks to me like an energy shock rather than an economy running too hot.

Why that matters

Higher interest rates can't make oil cheaper. They work by cooling demand, which takes time and hurts households and businesses along the way. If the pressure is coming from energy, raising rates risks doing the pain without much of the gain.

What would change my mind

Services inflation is still 3.7% and unemployment has fallen to 4.6%. If higher fuel prices start feeding into wage demands, the picture changes, and a rise would make sense. So my view is a patient "hold for now", with the door left open.

I'll be watching the decision on 17 December with real interest, and I'll write a follow-up on whether I got it right. You can read the full note on my projects page.

Which inflation should a Taylor rule use?

15 Nov 2026

The Taylor rule is a simple formula for where a central bank's interest rate "should" be, based on inflation and how much spare capacity there is in the economy. I built a version for the UK, expecting a neat answer. Instead it taught me how much depends on a single choice.

The surprise

Using core inflation (2.8%), the rule suggests a rate of about 3.5 to 4.2%, right around today's Bank Rate of 4.00%. Using headline inflation (3.6%), the same rule says 4.7 to 5.4%. Same formula, same economy, a gap of more than a percentage point.

Why it matters

Headline inflation includes energy prices, which central banks can't control and which often fade on their own. Core inflation is a better guide to underlying pressure. Markets pricing several more rate rises seem closer to the headline answer, which suggests they expect the Bank not to look through the energy shock.
